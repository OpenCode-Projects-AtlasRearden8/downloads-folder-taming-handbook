Chapter 3: One-Decision Triage Rules

Sorting dies at decision-making. "Should I keep this?" is a question with a dozen follow-ups — what is it, where does it belong, will I need it, what if I don't — and an ADHD brain correctly senses that each file is secretly a small meeting. So we remove the meeting. Every rule in this chapter asks exactly one yes/no question, in a fixed order. You answer, you move the file, done.

Keep the Decision Card open while you sort; that's this chapter compressed to one page. Here is the full engine, including the why behind each gate.

The Six Gates

Ask the questions in order, and stop at the first Yes:

Gate 1: "Is it obviously junk?" → Trash

Screenshot-0041 of an app you uninstalled. Three copies of a ticket PDF whose QR code you've already used. That mystery .tmp file from March. If your gut says junk and the file has no receipt- or record-flavor, it's junk. No deliberation allowed. If doubt creeps in, it just failed Gate 1's spirit — move it on to Gate 2 like a normal adult, but fast.

Gate 2: "Have I already gotten everything out of it?" → Trash

This is where installers die. An .exe/.msi/.dmg/.zip-installer is a wrapping — once the app is installed, the wrapper has finished its entire lifetime of work. Uninstalling the app later does not require the installer (Chapter 5 covers the exceptions). Same for tickets you've scanned, forms you've submitted, and "print this" PDFs you printed.

Gate 3: "Do I need to find this later?" → Reference

Anything with a future lookup: insurance cards, manuals, that password-reset code you printed, recipes you'll cook twice, reboot steps. These go in Filing/Reference with a name that describes what you'll search for (Chapter 4). If it would take more than 30 seconds to re-find on the internet, it's worth keeping; if it's 10 seconds to re-find, it's trash wearing a costume.

Gate 4: "Does something happen with this soon?" → Projects

An application with a deadline. An asset for the video you're editing this week. The lease to sign before Friday. Projects files get named by action (due-2026-11-04 rent application.pdf — yes, put dates in filenames; they sort beautifully) and live in Filing/Projects. When the action's done, the file re-triages in under five seconds: usually Gate 1 or Gate 3.

Gate 5: "Must I keep this, but rarely open it?" → Archive

Tax records, contracts, old invoices with legal flavor, receipts you promised your future self. Filing/Archive (optionally zipped once a year). You should never visit Archive weekly, only "yearly or in a panic."

Gate 6: None of the above → Inbox-unsorted, no guilt

One folder. Everything uncertain. It will be re-triaged at the next reset when you know its context better. The catch-all is a parole system, not a jail.

Speed tricks that keep it to one decision

  • Sort by type, not chronology. Downloads → show as list → group by type. Seeing all 28 PDFs together lets you judge the category once instead of file-by-file.
  • Power-select serial copies. Search the folder for -copy, -2, (1), and final. Duplicate-name patterns surface whole families of thrash to delete in one sweep (see Chapter 5 for duplicates specifically).
  • Never open a file to decide. Opening is quicksand. Judge by name, type, size, and modified date. If it truly can't be judged by exterior, it goes to Inbox-unsorted — the decision was never one-decision, so it's not this session's decision.
  • Keyboard first. Cut, arrow, paste. Mouse-heavy sorting is slower and — worse for us — more interesting. Interest invites rabbit holes.

What the rules are optimizing

Not perfect filing. A folder you're not afraid to open. A Downloads folder with 12 uncertain-but-contained files in Inbox-unsorted is a success. Zero files and zero certainty isn't required; zero fear is. When you feel that shift — opening Downloads and just... handling it — the system is working.
