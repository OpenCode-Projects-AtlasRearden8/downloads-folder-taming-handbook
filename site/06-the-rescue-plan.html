Chapter 6: The Rescue Plan

Somehow it's been a busy year and a half. Downloads has 4,000 items. Desktop Opening either folder produces a physical sigh. Good news: this is recoverable, with zero shame, using the same rules you already know — just applied in a different order and at a different pace.

The rescue mindset: you are not organizing 4,000 files, you are organizing categories. No file will ever be looked at individually unless it forces itself on you.

Step 0: Setup (5 min, one-time)

  • Make the three homes under Documents/Filing: Reference, Projects, Archive, plus the catch-all Inbox-unsorted.
  • Set the folder views: large icons OFF, list view ON, sorted by type — then modified date. Close the preview pane. (Details, not drama.)
  • Set a timer. In rescue mode it's not ten minutes; a rescue session is 30–45 minutes max, once a day. It's a nursing shift you scheduled, not a permanent job.

Step 1: Skim the category sizes (10 min)

Sort downloads by type and just look:

  • Installers and archives (.exe, .msi, .dmg, .pkg, .zip, .7z, .tar.gz) — usually 30–60% of count and size. Almost the entire mass can go to trash, per Chapter 5.
  • Images and screenshots (IMG_*, Screenshot *, *copy*) — usually huge count, small value.
  • Documents (.pdf, .docx) — small count, high value. Spend your attention here, not on the images.
  • Media and misc — usually photos, music, videos: only the few high-value

The insight: the biggest pile is almost always the least valuable. Clear the installer mountain first, and the task goes from "rescue" to "sorting" in one afternoon.

Step 2: The Installer Mountain Pass (30 min)

Sort by type. Select all the .exe/.zip/.dmg installers. Ask ONE question of the group: "did I already install this?" For almost every file the answer is yes. Move the whole group to Inbox-unsorted/installers-pending-delete-<today's-date>/, then bulk-delete a week later — this gives you a safety buffer without making you delete 200 files in one emotional day. Physical distance works on feelings too.

Step 3: The Image Sweep (30 min)

Sort images by date. Use the tile view to see them big. You're not deciding "keep or delete" per photo; you're sweeping "eras": each month is a mini-customer group. In each month, look for the three things worth keeping: (1) documents photographed as images — receipts, IDs, whiteboards — those are Reference, not photos; (2) memories you'd mourn; (3) anything that's the only copy of something. Everything else: select-all → delete, month by month.

Step 4: The Document Pond (30–45 min)

Now the valuable tail. Sort PDFs/docs by date, oldest first. Old documents triage themselves almost always into Gate 3 (Reference) or Gate 5 (Archive). Name the ones that matter (Chapter 4), move them home, done. This is real work — that's why it comes last, when the emotional weight is gone and the timer is your friend.

Step 5: Close out and set the cadence (10 min)

  • Move whatever Inbox-unsorted still holds — that stays.
  • Schedule the weekly reset now, this second, while the emotional cost of the mess is fresh. That's what prevents Rescue Plan 2.
  • If rescue went badly for a folder (still > 1,000 items), do not repeat it the next day. Wait a week, then do a smaller session. Rescue is a marathon, and grinding until you hate the project is how the mess comes back with friends.

If you panic mid-rescue

Stop, don't undo anything, and just relocate a copy of everything to Inbox-unsorted/rescue-snapshot — if a week later you can't find something you need, your safety net is there. Then never open the snapshot again unless you're actually missing a file. (This trick is a placebo that lets you delete boldly, and it works.)
