# Chapter 1: Why It Piles Up

If your Downloads folder and Desktop look like a landfill, it's not a character
flaw. Those two folders are *designed to accumulate*, and your brain is designed
to notice shiny things sitting nearby. Let's look at why the mess attaches
itself to exactly these folders — because knowing the mechanics makes the fix
feel less like self-improvement homework and more like fixing a machine.

## Downloads is a conveyor belt, not a shelf

Downloads has one job: it's the landing pad for everything the internet shovels
at you — PDFs, installers, invoices, that font you tried in 2023, forty copies
of `resume-v2-FINAL-final.docx`. Your browser doesn't ask where to put a file;
it just drops it on the belt.

The belt never stops on its own. So the pile grows until it becomes invisible —
a psychological trick called **tidal inundation** (see the
[Glossary](glossary.md)). Once the pile is big enough, opening the folder
produces a mild distress signal in your brain, so you stop opening it, so the
pile grows faster. It's a feedback loop, and it isn't your fault. Nobody handed
you a way off the belt.

Desktop's dynamic is different.

## Desktop is a low shelf facing the room

Desktop is the one place on your computer where files are *always visible*.
That's exactly why it fills up: visible means findable, so your brain learns
"leave it here and I'll see it later." Each screenshot, downloaded ticket, and
half-project you park there feels like leaving a note to yourself.

The problem: after approximately thirty items, visibility stops working. A
jungle of icons gives you the same findable-ness as a haystack. The shelf is
facing the room — but the room is full.

## The ADHD layer (without the moralizing)

Executive function is your brain's project manager: it starts tasks, holds
steps in mind, and closes them out. Filing a download requires all three:
decide *what it is*, decide *where it belongs*, *do the move*. That's three
sub-tasks for a file you encountered *one second ago*. Multiply that by
hundreds of files arriving with zero context, and the folder becomes a landfill
of postponed micro-decisions.

So the mess isn't "laziness." It's the gap between how folders want to be
organized and how attention actually arrives. The fix is to make the
postponement *structured* instead of chaotic — which is exactly what the next
chapters build.

## The three homes that fix it

Almost every file you'll ever download belongs in one of three places — and
none of them is Desktop:

| Home | What it's for | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reference** | Things you'll need to *find* again | insurance PDF, reboot steps, receipts |
| **Projects** | Things you need to *act on* soon | permit application, tax folder |
| **Archive** | Things you must *keep* but rarely *open* | tax returns, contracts |

Anything that fits none of those is almost certainly junk, and its destination
is the Recycle Bin. Chapter 3 turns that into literal one-decision rules.

Two things make the homes work:

1. **They live in one folder** (e.g. `Documents/Filing`), so there's only ever
   one place to look. Desktop keeps *at most* half a dozen open-project files.
2. **The weekly reset (Chapter 2)** closes the loop so the pile never gets a
   head start.

The rest of this handbook is just the details, done kindly.
